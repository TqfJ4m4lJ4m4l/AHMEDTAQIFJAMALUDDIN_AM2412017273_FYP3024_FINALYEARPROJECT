Understanding neurodivergence

Neurodivergence is a broad, nonmedical term. This guide explains what people mean by it, why the term is used, and how to think about traits and support without assuming everyone has the same experience.

Different ways of thinking and experiencing the world

What Is Neurodivergence?

Neurodivergent is a nonmedical term for people whose brains develop or work differently in ways that can affect how they think, learn, communicate, or experience their surroundings.

What does it mean to be neurodivergent?

People who are neurodivergent may have different strengths, preferences, and challenges. These experiences vary from person to person. A word or diagnosis cannot tell you everything about someone, and people with the same diagnosis can have different needs.

The related word neurodiversity describes the natural variety in how human brains work. People who do not identify as neurodivergent are sometimes described as neurotypical.

Why use this term?

People use the term to talk about brain differences without treating one way of thinking as the only “normal” way. It can help people describe their experiences and discuss support, access, and inclusion.

Using the term does not mean that a person has no difficulties or should not receive care. A person can value their identity and still want help with challenges that affect their wellbeing or daily life.

Is being neurodivergent a disability?

Some neurodivergent people consider themselves disabled; others may not. A person’s experience can depend on their individual needs and on whether their surroundings provide suitable access and accommodations. The person’s own language and preferences matter.

There is no single neurodivergent experience

Ask what a person prefers and what support would help. Do not assume their abilities, challenges, or support needs from a label.

Possible causes, experiences, and support

Traits and Approaches

Possible causes

Neurodivergence is not one medical condition, so it does not have one single cause. The factors associated with a particular difference or diagnosis vary. They may involve development, genetics, health, or a combination of factors, and a specific cause may not be known.

A person’s traits are not proof of a diagnosis. Only a qualified healthcare professional can assess a health or developmental condition.

Possible traits and symptoms

There is no universal symptom list for being neurodivergent. Traits depend on the individual and, where relevant, the specific condition. Some people may experience differences involving:

  • Attention, concentration, or switching between tasks.
  • Reading, writing, spelling, or working with numbers.
  • Communication, social interaction, or understanding social expectations.
  • Sensory experiences, such as sound, light, touch, or busy environments.
  • Movement, coordination, routines, or planning.

These examples are not a checklist. Having one or more of these experiences does not by itself mean someone has a particular condition.

Conditions and differences often discussed under this term

People and communities use “neurodivergent” in different ways. Conditions and differences often discussed in this context include:

  • Autism: a developmental condition that can affect communication, interaction, routines, interests, and sensory experiences.
  • ADHD: a developmental condition that can affect attention, activity, and impulse control. Its presentation can be primarily inattentive, primarily hyperactive-impulsive, or combined.
  • ADD: an older term that is still used informally. ADHD presentations that are primarily inattentive are described today under ADHD.
  • Dyslexia: a learning difference commonly associated with reading and written language.
  • Dyscalculia: a learning difference involving numbers and mathematical tasks.
  • Dysgraphia: a term used for significant difficulty with writing skills.
  • Developmental coordination disorder (dyspraxia): a condition that can affect movement and coordination.
  • Tourette syndrome: a neurological condition involving tics.

Some people also use the term for other developmental, learning, neurological, or mental health conditions. There is no single agreed list, and not everyone with a diagnosis chooses to identify as neurodivergent.

Approaches that can help

Support is most useful when it is discussed with the person and adjusted to their preferences. Helpful approaches may include:

  • Use clear language and explain unfamiliar words or steps.
  • Allow time to process information and respond.
  • Offer written information or another communication option.
  • Ask about sensory preferences, such as lighting, sound, or quiet spaces.
  • Explain changes to plans in advance when possible.
  • Offer choices and include the person in decisions that affect them.

Finding out more

If you are concerned about your own experiences or someone else’s, consider speaking with a qualified healthcare provider. They can discuss whether an assessment or other support may be useful.

Personalised care and practical support

Medication and Support

There is no medication that changes neurodivergent identity. Support depends on the person and on any condition or health concern they want help with.

Medication may be considered by a qualified clinician for some co-occurring conditions or specific concerns. The decision should be individual, with the person informed about the expected benefits, possible risks, and follow-up.

Medication advice is individual

Do not start, stop, or change medication based only on this page. Discuss questions about medication with the prescribing clinician.

Support can include

  • Reasonable adjustments at home, school, work, or appointments.
  • Communication support that respects the person’s preferred way to communicate.
  • Help with related needs, such as sleep, anxiety, or mental health.
  • Support for families and caregivers when the person wants it.
  • Services chosen with the person and reviewed as their needs change.
